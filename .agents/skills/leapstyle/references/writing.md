# LEAP writing and editing standard

Use this reference to draft or revise academic prose. It governs rhetoric, not the substance of the argument. Preserve the author's claims, qualifications, evidence, citations, technical definitions and uncertainty. If revision exposes a possible error, report it separately rather than repairing the argument silently.

## 1. Editorial contract

### Preserve content

An edit must not silently:

- add or remove a claim;
- strengthen causality, generality, certainty or novelty;
- change a definition, mathematical condition or empirical result;
- remove an exception, qualification or limitation;
- invent evidence, citations, mechanisms or motives;
- change a quotation;
- alter a number, sample, date, label, reference or equation;
- substitute the editor's preferred argument for the author's argument.

Style changes can alter syntax, order and emphasis only when propositional content survives. When a sentence appears false, unsupported, inconsistent or too strong, keep the manuscript faithful and record the concern in an argument note with the relevant passage.

### Respect genre and venue

Follow explicit journal, publisher, funder or institutional instructions first. Otherwise use the defaults below. Preserve discipline-specific terms of art and notation when substitution would reduce accuracy.

### Prefer literal clarity

The prose should earn attention through the argument. Do not make it sound important by adding grand claims, inflated adjectives or ornamental metaphor.

## 2. Order of work

Revise from large to small:

1. state the paper's question, answer and contribution in plain language;
2. map the argumentative function of each section;
3. repair section order and transitions;
4. repair paragraph logic;
5. improve sentences, diction and rhythm;
6. audit citations, numbers, qualifications and cross-references;
7. perform a cold read of the rendered document.

Do not polish a sentence whose paragraph may be deleted or moved.

For a full manuscript, first make a short reverse outline. Each paragraph should have one identifiable job. Repetition is justified only when it serves a new rhetorical function, such as preview, demonstration or synthesis.

## 3. Argument and paragraph structure

### Lead with the intellectual problem

Give the reader a reason to care about the question before providing institutional detail or a literature inventory. State the answer early enough that the reader can understand why later sections matter.

### Make the contribution relational

Explain what the paper changes relative to what a reasonable reader would otherwise believe. Avoid empty priority claims such as `the first paper to`. A contribution is stronger when its consequence is clear.

### Build paragraphs around claims

A strong paragraph normally has:

1. a sentence that states its local claim or purpose;
2. evidence, reasoning or qualification;
3. a final sentence that establishes the implication or transition.

This is a diagnostic pattern, not a template to repeat mechanically. Vary paragraph length and openings. Do not begin successive paragraphs with identical signposts.

### Use explicit transitions

A transition should name the logical relation: cause, contrast, qualification, sequence, example, implication or return. Avoid decorative connectors such as `moreover` when the relationship is not additive.

Use backward links when a new section depends on a result already established. Use forward promises only when the promised material appears soon and matters to the present inference.

### Keep the agent visible

Prefer sentences in which the reader can identify who acts, infers, observes or assumes. Passive voice is useful when the object or procedure deserves emphasis, but habitual passive voice obscures responsibility.

In economics, prefer first person (`we` or `I`) to sustained references to `this paper`. Use `this paper` occasionally when it improves variety. Prefer active descriptions such as `we estimate` to habitual passive constructions such as `regressions are estimated`.

## 4. Sentence design and rhythm

### Put weight in the right place

- Put familiar context before new information.
- Keep subjects close to their verbs.
- Put qualifications beside the claim they qualify.
- Place the sentence's most consequential words near the end.
- Move long conditions or definitions out of the subject position.

### Vary length deliberately

Use short sentences for a hinge, conclusion or correction. Use medium sentences for most exposition. Use long sentences when hierarchy matters and the clauses remain easy to parse. A page of uniformly medium sentences is accurate but lifeless; a page of short sentences is breathless.

Read difficult passages aloud. Revise when stress falls on function words, successive sentences have the same cadence or punctuation is doing work that syntax should do.

### Prefer verbs to nominalisations

- `we analyse` rather than `we conduct an analysis of`;
- `prices fell` rather than `there was a decline in prices`;
- `the model assumes` rather than `the assumption of the model is`.

Keep nominalisations that are genuine technical concepts.

### Remove throat-clearing

Delete openings such as `it is important to note that`, `it should be emphasised that`, `in order to` and `the fact that` when the sentence remains accurate without them.

### Control parallelism

Use parallel grammatical forms for comparable items. Do not force a list of three for rhetorical neatness. Repeated triplets quickly become mannered and can conceal distinctions among the items.

### Limit antithetical formulas

Use `not X but Y` no more than twice in a manuscript unless the exact contrast is indispensable. Vary or state other contrasts directly. Do not build a paper from serial reversals.

## 5. Diction and technical terms

### Choose concrete, exact words

Prefer the shortest word that preserves the intended meaning. Replace vague abstractions such as `factors`, `aspects`, `dynamics` and `issues` when the manuscript can name the actual variable, institution, action or mechanism.

Avoid promotional words including `groundbreaking`, `novel`, `remarkable`, `crucial` and `important` unless the sentence demonstrates the evaluation. Let the consequence establish importance.

### Define terms on first use

Give unfamiliar technical terms a compact natural-language gloss at first use. A gloss should orient the intended reader without interrupting specialists. For example:

```text
cue-dependent retrieval, the recovery of information in response to a prompt,
```

Do not repeatedly redefine the term. Do not replace an exact term with an approximate everyday synonym after it has been defined.

### Use abbreviations sparingly

Introduce an abbreviation only when it recurs enough to reduce effort. Spell it out at first use and avoid clusters of local abbreviations that force the reader to memorise a codebook.

### Avoid fashionable filler

Scrutinise words such as `lens`, `space`, `navigate`, `unpack`, `foreground`, `leverage`, `robust`, `nuanced`, `complex` and `dynamic`. Retain them only when they express a precise technical meaning.

## 6. Punctuation, spelling and mechanics

### Use UK English by default

Unless the venue or source document requires another convention, use UK spelling:

- `labour`, `behaviour`, `colour`;
- `organise`, `characterise`, `generalise`;
- `centre`, `licence` as a noun and `practice` as a noun.

Apply spelling consistently in the author's prose. Do not alter titles, quotations, proper names, code, commands or bibliography fields merely to enforce house spelling.

### Do not use em dashes

Do not insert em dashes. Replace them with commas, parentheses, a colon, a semicolon or a new sentence according to the logical relation. In LaTeX, audit prose for `---`.

Use an en dash for ranges and conventional paired terms where appropriate. In LaTeX this is `--`, preferably without surrounding spaces for numeric ranges.

### Avoid the serial comma by default

Write `A, B and C` unless the final comma is needed to prevent a genuine misreading or the venue requires it. Do not remove a comma when doing so creates ambiguity.

### Use semicolons only for structure

Use a semicolon between closely related independent clauses or to separate list items that already contain commas. Do not use it merely to make a sentence look formal.

### Use parentheses for subordinate information

If the parenthetical material carries the main claim, integrate it into the sentence. Avoid nested parentheses.

### Keep quotation marks and apostrophes consistent

Follow the project's existing LaTeX convention. Do not alter quoted material. Place punctuation according to the chosen editorial convention unless the punctuation belongs to a quoted expression or code.

### Use footnotes sparingly

Aim for no footnotes in the introduction where possible and no more than about one footnote per page in the paper as a general rule. Delete an avoidable footnote, integrate essential content into the text or move it to the relevant body section. The most defensible introduction footnotes usually identify tangentially related literature in the contribution paragraphs.

## 7. Evidence, citations and claims

### Match scope to evidence

Use causal language only when the design or argument supports it. Distinguish:

- evidence from interpretation;
- a necessary condition from a sufficient condition;
- model implications from empirical facts;
- absence of evidence from evidence of absence;
- measured performance from the underlying capacity being measured;
- one domain or sample from a general proposition.

When the evidence supports a conditional conclusion, state the condition in the claim rather than burying it later.

### Attach citations to the proposition they support

Place citations close enough that the reader can see their scope. A citation at the end of a long paragraph should not appear to support every preceding sentence unless it does.

Do not add a citation from memory. Verify the source before adding it. Do not remove citations as redundant without checking whether they support distinct claims.

Every reference-list entry carries the authors' full given names, not initials. When a `.bib` entry has only initials, resolve it against a source or ask -- never expand an initial from memory, and never infer a first name from an initial and a surname. The procedure is in [paper.md](paper.md) under "Full first names, and never inventing one".

Keep citations sparse in the first few introduction paragraphs and avoid them in the opening paragraph where possible. When an early motivational or methodological precedent is useful, one representative source introduced by `e.g.` is usually enough. Put most introduction citations in the contribution paragraphs and explain the precise relationship between each cited work and the paper.

Describe prior work neutrally. Prefer `builds on`, `extends` and `complements` to claims that earlier studies `failed` or have `little value`. Use a priority claim such as `We are the first to ...` only after verifying it, and state why the contribution matters.

### Introduce quotations

Explain why a quotation is present and what follows from it. Avoid leaving quotations to make the argument unaided. Preserve wording and page references exactly unless checked against the source.

### Calibrate reporting verbs

Use `shows`, `finds`, `estimates`, `argues`, `documents`, `suggests` and `is consistent with` according to what the source actually establishes. Do not turn `suggests` into `shows` for energy.

## 8. Figurative language and rhetorical devices

Use figurative language only when it clarifies a relation more precisely than literal prose. Avoid decorative metaphors, mixed metaphors and extended analogies that introduce implications the argument does not support.

Terms of art with figurative origins may remain when they are conventional and exact. Explain them if the intended audience may read them literally.

Rhetorical questions should be genuine questions that organise inquiry, not a device for announcing an obvious answer. One well-placed question is usually stronger than a succession of them.

Avoid excessive alliteration, slogans, one-sentence paragraphs and repeated sentence fragments. These devices attract attention to the writer rather than the inference.

Humour may lower the cost of attention, but it must not belittle subjects, evidence or readers. Never use wit to conceal a weak step in the argument.

## 9. Abstracts, introductions and conclusions

### Abstract

An abstract should usually identify:

1. the problem or question;
2. the paper's approach;
3. the principal result or claim;
4. the implication and its limit.

Do not begin with generic statements about the importance of a broad topic. Avoid citations unless the venue or argument requires them. Ensure every claim in the abstract appears, with the same scope, in the paper.

### Introduction

**The rule the rest of this formula serves: say what the paper does in paragraph 2.** Everything below is subordinate to that. If the reader has to reach paragraph 4 to learn what was done, the introduction has failed regardless of how well the other paragraphs are written.

Treat the following paragraph sequence as the LEAP default for an economics paper, not as a universal law. Follow a venue or paper-specific structure when it is more appropriate, but do not simulate concision by making the prescribed paragraphs unusually long.

1. **Big-picture motivation and knowledge gap.** Explain why the question matters and state clearly what we do not yet know. Keep this paragraph to no more than half a page and preferably to a third of a page or less.
2. **What the paper does.** Describe the main approach in one paragraph. Use two only when the approach is genuinely complicated. Give enough detail for the reader to understand and assess the method, but avoid a data inventory or detailed identification discussion. If the design uses an instrument, name it and explain briefly why it is valid. Do not preview extensions, heterogeneity or mechanisms here.
3. **What the paper finds.** State the main findings in context, then explain what they imply and why they matter. Do not present an unconnected list of estimates. Summarise the most important robustness evidence concisely, for example: `Our findings are robust to [variations].`
4. **Extensions, heterogeneity and mechanisms.** Let the reader absorb the main result before adding these analyses. For closely related extensions, describe the analyses in one paragraph and their combined findings in the next. For distinct extensions, devote one paragraph to each and state what the paper does followed by what it finds. More than four paragraphs on extensions will rarely be optimal.
5. **Literature contributions.** Use no more than two paragraphs. Organise them around what the paper changes in the literature and why that change matters. Avoid a separate literature-review section unless the venue or subject requires one.
6. **Roadmap.** End with a brief paragraph beginning, where natural, `The rest of the paper is structured as follows.` Retain an informative roadmap because paper structures differ, particularly in PhD work. Make it predict the argumentative sequence rather than merely reciting section numbers.

Audit the introduction against these common failures:

- Reach what the paper does in paragraph 2. A long motivational preamble weakens the introduction.
- Name the missing knowledge explicitly. When relevant, explain what previously prevented an answer, such as unavailable data, absent exogenous variation or computational constraints, and how the paper overcomes that obstacle.
- Avoid a strawman question whose qualitative answer is already accepted. Ask a quantitative question or explain the competing forces that make the answer uncertain.
- State contributions as consequences, not merely differences. `Doe et al. (2012) do X, whereas we do Y` is incomplete until the introduction explains why Y changes what readers know.
- Keep the focus on this paper. Compress accounts of prior work and frame comparisons around the present contribution.
- Cite methodological precedents selectively. Discuss the broader methodological literature in the empirical-strategy section.
- Put citations in context. Explain whether the paper builds on, extends, complements or differs from the cited work and why that relationship matters.
- Interpret results. Connect estimates to the motivating question, knowledge gap or policy problem rather than leaving the reader with coefficients alone.
- Balance detail by asking what readers need to understand and assess the paper. Omit minor data description, secondary results and routine checks. Include the source and logic of identification, key responses to obvious objections and a few informative robustness or placebo checks.
- Remove material that is not directly relevant to the research question, even when it connects loosely to an outcome or policy in the paper.
- Keep footnotes out of the introduction. A footnote interrupts the one part of the paper that most needs to read straight through. The defensible exception is a note in the contribution paragraphs identifying tangentially related literature.

When styling a full manuscript, audit the introduction against this formula and report the result: which paragraph does which job, which slot is missing, and which is out of order. Do not silently rearrange an author's introduction; show the mapping and propose the repair.

### Conclusion

The introduction earns the reader's attention; the conclusion decides what they carry away. Write it in the register expected by the *Journal of Political Economy* or the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*: three to five paragraphs, roughly 600 to 900 words, and never longer than the introduction.

1. **The answer.** Name the question and answer it, in one or two sentences, at exactly the level the evidence supports. Not `this paper examined`. This paragraph should survive on its own: if a reader quoted only it, they would have the paper's result.
2. **What the number means.** Put the headline estimate into a unit a reader can price -- a share of a benchmark, a comparison with a known effect, a back-of-envelope aggregate. This is the paragraph in which the result stops being a coefficient and becomes a fact about the world.
3. **What changes in what we believed.** State the revision to priors relationally: what a reader who held the standard view should now believe instead, and why that matters. `Consistent with the literature` says nothing. Name the belief the paper moves.
4. **Where the result binds.** Scope, not apology. Give the population, period and margin over which the estimate identifies something, then name the nearest setting where it would not hold and say why. Policy implications, if the paper has any, belong here, stated at the scope the design supports with the condition attached.
5. **The open question.** Name the data, variation or experiment that would settle what remains. A research design, not a genre sentence.

Paragraphs 2 and 3 may merge in a short paper; paragraph 4 may merge with 5. Paragraph 1 never merges with anything.

Hard rules:

- No new evidence. No number, table, figure or robustness result appears in the conclusion that is not already in the body.
- No section-by-section summary (`Section 2 described the data...`).
- No replay of the abstract sentence by sentence.
- No `In conclusion`, `To conclude`, `This paper has shown that`, `Further research is needed`.
- No bullet lists.
- No claim wider or narrower in scope than the same claim in the results section. The conclusion is where overclaiming happens; check it against the results, not against the abstract.

Audit the conclusion against these questions:

- Does the first paragraph answer the title's question in one sentence?
- Does the headline number appear with a unit and a benchmark?
- Is there a sentence saying what a reader should now believe that they did not before?
- Does any number appear that is not already in the body? There must be none.
- Is the final paragraph a specific research design rather than a genre sentence?
- Is the conclusion shorter than the introduction?

### No limitations section, ever

Never give a paper a separate `Limitations`, `Caveats`, `Shortcomings` or `Scope conditions` section, and never a subsection either. A limitations section quarantines the honest content of a paper in the one place a referee reads as a confession, and it removes each limitation from the point where the reader actually needs it.

Fold every limitation into the section where the reader meets the problem:

| The limitation | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Measurement error, coverage, selection into the source, transcription and linkage error | Data |
| Threats to the identifying assumption, and the tests that address each one | Empirical strategy |
| Statistical power, interpretation of magnitudes, competing explanations for the estimate | Results |
| External validity and the scope of the finding | Conclusion, paragraph 4 |

A limitation stated where the reader meets it reads as command of the material. The same sentence in a limitations section reads as an apology.

Mechanical audit: flag any section or subsection heading matching `limitation`, `caveat`, `shortcoming` or `scope condition`, and any paragraph opening `A limitation of this study`, `One caveat` or `This study is not without`. Relocate the content according to the table and report each move.

## 10. Validation and handoff

### Mechanical audit

Compare the edited version with the source. Check, as applicable:

- citation-key multiset;
- numbers, dates, percentages and monetary amounts;
- quotations and page references;
- labels, references, equations, propositions and table entries;
- section inventory and disclosed limitations;
- headings matching `limitation`, `caveat`, `shortcoming` or `scope condition`, which must not exist;
- introduction paragraph sequence and footnote density;
- conclusion paragraph sequence, and any number in the conclusion that is absent from the body;
- reference-list entries carrying initials rather than full given names;
- spelling convention;
- em dashes (`---` in LaTeX prose);
- repeated `not ... but ...` constructions;
- repeated three-item lists or adjective triplets;
- undefined abbreviations and unglossed technical terms.

Exclude commands, URLs, bibliography data and mathematical minus signs from punctuation searches where appropriate.

### Content-drift audit

For each substantial rewrite, ask:

1. Is the agent unchanged?
2. Is the action or relation unchanged?
3. Is the degree of certainty unchanged?
4. Is the domain and time period unchanged?
5. Are exceptions and conditions still present?
6. Is causal language no stronger?
7. Could a reasonable reader draw a new inference from the revision?

If the last answer is yes, restore the source meaning or flag the change for the author.

### Cold read

Read the rendered document without consulting the source. Mark sentences that require rereading, paragraphs whose purpose is unclear and transitions that presume unstated logic. Then compare every proposed repair with the source before accepting it.

### Handoff

Report:

- the output file and whether the source was preserved;
- the principal rhetorical improvements;
- the validation performed;
- any unresolved layout or citation warnings;
- possible errors or overclaims discovered during editing, kept separate from the manuscript.
